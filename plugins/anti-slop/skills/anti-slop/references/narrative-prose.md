## Dialogue attribution

`said` is invisible and correct. Every substitute draws attention to the tag
instead of the line.

Never: `murmured` · `chuckled` · `exclaimed` · `interjected` · `chimed` ·
`stammered` · `grumbled` · `echoed` · `regaled` · `commiserated`

`chuckles darkly` is the single most recognizable tag on the slop list.

## Adverbs of manner

`conspiratorially` · `quizzically` · `appreciatively` · `approvingly` ·
`disapprovingly` · `sympathetically` · `understandingly` · `questioningly` ·
`reassuringly` · `mischievously` · `enigmatically` · `triumphantly` ·
`somberly` · `worriedly` · `skeptically` · `hesitantly` · `nervously` ·
`excitedly` · `eagerly` · `warmly` · `cheerily` · `animatedly` ·
`absentmindedly` · `blankly` · `intently` · `thoughtfully` ·
`contemplatively` · `considerately` · `supportively` · `remorsefully` ·
`sagely` · `gruffly` · `naturedly`

> "'You're late,' she said accusingly." → "'You're late.' She did not sit down."

## Body-language clichés

Ban the whole phrase, not the noun.

`barely above a whisper` · `barely a whisper` · `voice was soft and gentle` ·
`shivers down / up the spine` · `eyes glinted / sparkled / twinkled` ·
`eyes sparkling with mischief` · `eyes never leaving` · `chestnut eyes` ·
`long lashes` · `cheeks flaming` · `cheeks hollowing` · `furrowed brow` ·
`steepled fingers` · `unshed tears` · `for what seemed like an eternity` ·
`with a mixture of X and Y` · `torn between` · `a mix of` · `despite herself`

High-frequency verbs from the same family — fine once per chapter, tells in
clusters: `leaned` · `nodded` · `glanced` · `sighed` · `paused` · `hesitated` ·
`fidgeted` · `hunched` · `huddled` · `gestured` · `murmured` · `flickered`

## Chapter-end cadences

Generated chapters converge on the same handful of closing beats.

`for now, that was enough` · `maybe that was enough` · `maybe, just maybe` ·
`perhaps, just perhaps` · `little did he know` · `life would never be the same` ·
`was only just beginning` · `only just getting started` ·
`they would face it together` · `the ball is in your court` ·
`the choice is yours` · `once upon a time`

## LLM stock names and places

Models reach for the same invented proper nouns across unrelated prompts. Any of
these in a draft means the name was generated, not chosen.

**People:** Elara · Elysia · Elianore · Aria · Lyra · Kael · Aldric · Eadric ·
Zara · Amara · Anika · Ayla · Freydis · Eluned · Eira

**Places:** Eldoria · Ravenswood · Oakhaven · Maplewood · Whisperwood ·
Seabrook · Bakersville · Greenhaven · Zephyria · Numeria · Atheria · Melodia ·
Technopolis

In a licensed universe this matters twice: a stock name is both a slop tell and
a canon violation. Take names from the source material.

## Metaphor shells

Same bans as business prose, and they hit harder in fiction because they replace
an image the scene could have earned.

`tapestry` · `symphony` · `kaleidoscope` · `labyrinth` · `orchestra of` ·
`dance of` · `rivulets of` · `cacophony` · `gossamer` · `ethereal beauty` ·
`bore silent witness to` · `air was filled with anticipation` ·
`moth to a flame` · `leaving trails of fire` · `threatens to consume`

## What to do instead

1. **Cut the adverb, keep the line.** If the dialogue needs the adverb to land,
   the dialogue is the problem.
2. **Replace a body-language cliché with a physical fact** specific to this
   character in this room — what they do with their hands, what they look at.
3. **End the chapter one sentence earlier.** Slop cadences are almost always the
   sentence after the real ending.
4. **Check names against the source**, never against invention.

Beyond the word list, additionally check by hand: weak dialogue, tell-don't-show,
unsurprising or uncreative, amateurish, purple prose, forced poetry or metaphor,
and *degradation* (whether chapter 8 is weaker than chapter 1 — the failure mode
specific to long generated prose).
